You Asked Them to Delete Your Data. What Happens Next?

California’s DROP system makes privacy requests easier. Finding the right records is still part of the challenge.

SP
Sakshat PatilFollow
6 min read · Nov 3, 2026

You delete an app, clear your browsing history, and move on. Somewhere else, information collected during those activities may still exist.

A data broker might hold your email address, shopping habits, browsing history, or an estimate of your interests. You may never have created an account with that company. You may not even recognize its name.

California’s Delete Request and Opt-out Platform, known as DROP, gives residents a simpler way to respond. Instead of tracking down brokers individually, they can submit one request through a free state service. But sending a request and removing information are separate steps. Between them sits a less visible problem: connecting the request to the right records.

A profile you never created

Imagine Maya, a college student in California, searching for hiking routes and buying a pair of trail shoes. These are ordinary activities. In a fictional data profile, however, they might become evidence that she is an “outdoor enthusiast.”

That label could be useful to an advertiser, even if Maya bought the shoes for someone else.

Data brokers combine information from different sources and make inferences about people. CalPrivacy lists shopping habits, precise location, browsing history, and health-related information among the categories brokers may collect and sell. The resulting profile can contain both recorded activities and assumptions drawn from them. privacy.ca.gov

How do you ask a company to remove your information when you do not know it has any?

Privacy rights can become homework

Before a shared request system, exercising privacy rights could mean finding individual brokers, locating their forms, and following different instructions for each one.

Research suggests that submitting a request did not reliably produce a response. In Consumer Beware! Exploring Data Brokers’ CCPA Compliance, researchers submitted data-access requests to 543 California-registered brokers. More than 40 percent did not respond. Some responding companies requested additional personal information for identity verification, creating another concern for people trying to protect their privacy. arxiv.org

The study examined access requests before DROP processing began. Its findings do not establish how well DROP deletes information. They do show why a right can be difficult to use when the person exercising it must navigate hundreds of separate processes.

DROP addresses that burden at the point of entry. California residents verify eligibility, create a profile, and submit a request through one service. Brokers began processing requests in August 2026, and status updates can take up to 90 days. privacy.ca.gov

Which version of you is in the database?

Return to Maya. She now uses her university email address, but an older record might contain the email she used in high school. Another broker might identify her through a phone number.

The person is the same. The identifiers are different.

This fictional example illustrates why matching matters. A broker must connect information in a request with information in its own records. CalPrivacy explains that different brokers may hold different details and that providing additional identifiers can improve the likelihood of finding a match. privacy.ca.gov

This creates a practical decision for users: how much information should they provide to help locate information they want removed?

A simple submission form can reduce effort, but it cannot make every record equally easy to identify.

Read the response carefully

DROP’s response categories help explain why the outcome matters as much as the request.

DeletedThe broker matched the person and removed information that is not exempt.
ExemptedThe information can legally be retained.
PendingProcessing has not been completed.
Opted-outNo exact match. The broker keeps the data for now but cannot sell or share it.
Record not foundThe broker may have no information, or the details provided were not enough to locate a record.

“Record not found” requires particular care. It may mean the broker has no information about the person. It may also mean the information provided was insufficient to locate a record. “Opted-out” is another distinct outcome: the broker could not make an exact match and retains data for now, but cannot sell or share it. privacy.ca.gov

A confirmation that a request was submitted therefore answers only the first question. The next question is what each broker did with it.

A useful start, with work behind it

DROP makes a complicated privacy task more manageable. Its value is straightforward: people should not need to become experts in the data-broker industry to exercise their rights.

Its limits also deserve attention. DROP does not erase every piece of information everywhere. Exemptions apply, and information given directly to a business falls outside the scope of a DROP request. Brokers must continue checking for new matching information after initial processing. privacy.ca.gov

For California residents, the practical starting point is the official DROP website. After submitting a request, save the DROP ID privately and review the reported outcomes.

The larger question is whether people can understand those outcomes. A privacy tool becomes more meaningful when users can see what was found, what was removed, and what remains unresolved.
